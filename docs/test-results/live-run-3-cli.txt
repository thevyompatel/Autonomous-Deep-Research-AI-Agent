                       Is Nuclear Power Necessary to Reach Net-Zero Emissions by 2050?                       

Executive summary                                                                                            

Nuclear power is not strictly designated as an absolute necessity in every single scenario, but the sources  
indicate that less nuclear power would make reaching net-zero emissions by 2050 significantly harder and more
expensive [4]. While renewable sources like solar and wind dominate net-zero pathways—such as the            
International Energy Agency's (IEA) Net Zero Emissions by 2050 Scenario (NZE), where renewables rise to      
nearly 90% of electricity supply—nuclear energy plays a crucial, supportive role by providing reliable       
baseload power, low-carbon electricity, grid stability, and new sources of flexibility and clean hydrogen    
[4][5][10][13].                                                                                              

The Role of Nuclear Power in Net-Zero Scenarios                                                              

In the IEA’s Net Zero Emissions by 2050 Scenario (NZE), global energy sector emissions decline to zero on a  
net basis by 2050 [4]. While renewable sources are projected to make up nearly 90% of electricity supply in  
this pathway, nuclear energy continues to play a significant role as an established, large-scale             
low-emissions energy source [4]. The nuclear industry currently provides about 9% of the world's electricity 
from roughly 440 power reactors and constitutes over 20% of the world's low-carbon electricity, making it the
second-largest source of low-carbon power globally [1].                                                      

Nuclear power plants boast exceptionally low lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions, comparable to wind, hydro,  
and biomass, and drastically lower than unabated fossil fuels [7][8]. Retaining or expanding nuclear capacity
is highlighted as a major tool for displacing unabated coal and avoiding billions of tons of CO2 emissions   
[2][5].                                                                                                      

Consequences of Reduced Nuclear Capacity                                                                     

Failing to accelerate nuclear construction and extend the lifetimes of existing reactors creates severe      
challenges for clean energy transitions [4]. The "Low Nuclear Case" variant of the IEA’s NZE demonstrates    
that if nuclear's share of total generation drops from 10% in 2020 to 3% in 2050, solar and wind would have  
to bridge the gap [4]. This outcome would push the frontiers of integrating variable renewables, require     
significantly more energy storage, and necessitate fossil fuel plants fitted with carbon capture,            
utilisation, and storage (CCS) [4]. Consequently, operating with less nuclear power makes achieving net-zero 
ambitions harder and more expensive [4].                                                                     

Furthermore, modeled costs for a 100% carbon-free electricity grid tend to be prohibitively high without the 
incorporation of dispatchable clean resources, such as advanced nuclear, or significant flexible demand [12].

Contributions Beyond Electricity Generation                                                                  

Beyond direct electricity generation to decarbonize grids, nuclear energy offers multi-faceted utility that  
supports net-zero targets across hard-to-abate sectors [5]. Nuclear plants can support:                      

 • Hydrogen Production: Surplus nuclear electricity can be utilized to produce millions of tonnes of         
   hydrogen, serving as an alternative energy carrier for industry and transportation [4][5][13].            
 • Industrial Process Heat: Nuclear facilities can co-generate heat to replace district heating and          
   high-temperature manufacturing uses [4][13].                                                              
 • Advanced Technologies: Emerging nuclear technologies, such as Small Modular Reactors (SMRs), provide      
   compact, flexible alternatives with enhanced safety features and lower investment costs, capable of       
   powering data centers amid the artificial intelligence boom, industrial processes, and remote areas       
   [13][14].                                                                                                 

Grid Reliability and Flexibility Challenges                                                                  

Transitioning away from fossil fuels while maintaining system reliability requires managing the intermittent 
nature of high shares of weather-dependent renewables [6][10][12]. While major grids already operate on high 
percentages of renewables, high-renewable systems demand highly flexible thermal capacity, massive           
investments in new generation capacity to meet peak demand, and diverse energy storage to maintain overall   
grid balance [10][12]. Nuclear power acts as a reliable baseload and dispatchable clean resource that can    
alleviate some of these scalability and peak-time reliability challenges [3][10][12][13].
